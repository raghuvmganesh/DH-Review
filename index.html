Digital Humanities (DH) Project Review

Names of both participants: Munir and Raghu
Website reviewed: https://www.past-india.com/

1. What is the website about?

The website acts as a digital archive dedicated to preserving India’s visual and cultural heritage through vintage mediums. It features historic photographs, postcards, Raja Ravi Varma oleographs, old paper prints, and ephemera that capture scenes of urban infrastructure, culture, and everyday life in pre- and post-independence India.

2. Was it clear what you had to do on the project? (Navigation/UX)

Yes, the platform is simple and straightforward to navigate. However, it functions largely as an unstructured repository filled with disparate information and artifacts centered on India's past.

3. What’s the main argument of the website? Is using it aligned with the goal?

The website lacks a strong overarching argument, narrative structure, or thesis. Instead of offering an analytical framework, it functions as a digital cabinet of curiosities—a virtual collection/museum displaying vintage paraphernalia without a central scholarly thesis.

4. What features of the project did you find interesting?

The visual collections stand out—specifically the original historical photographs, postcards, and prints detailing specific locations, events, and historical figures.

5. What is the source for the project data, and how has the author presented it?

The provenance and metadata of the sources are largely ambiguous. The primary sources appear to be scanned prints, digitized postcards, or photos taken via digital cameras/phones, presented with minimal archival provenance or academic citation.

DH Methodology & Critique

6. What are the 'parts' [Materials / Processing / Presentation] that make this a DH project?

  • Materials: Historical photographs, vintage post cards, paper prints, and artwork (such as Raja Ravi Varma oleographs).
  • Processing: Digitization through high-resolution scanning or photography, file compression, web upload, and categorization.
  • Presentation: A web-based gallery grid with post entries and text descriptions providing context for individual items.

7. What aspect of "Digital" is helping the project more than a "conventional" humanities project?

  • Preservation & Accessibility: Digitization allows users to view and study fragile artifacts repeatedly without physical wear and tear on original paper materials.
  • Global Access: It breaks physical barriers, allowing anyone across the world to access these historical visual records simultaneously.

8. What aspect of "Digital" is harming the project?

  • Loss of Materiality: The tangible qualities of the physical objects (paper texture, stamp impressions, physical scale, aging markers) are lost on a flat screen.
  • Impact on Physical Archives: Digitization can decrease physical footfall to regional museums and local archives, reducing ticket revenue needed to maintain physical collections.

9. What critiques from Digital Humanities discourse fit this project?

  • "Fancy Tools / Digital Platforms over Rigor": The project focuses on digital presentation and collection rather than critical contextualization, rigorous metadata standards, or scholarly analysis.
  • Uncritical Imperial Archives: The project features significant British Raj-era imagery without sufficient critical framing or decolonial analysis, risking the romanticization or perpetuation of colonial rule as a "period of glory" or nostalgia.
